# Example reports

These are real runs against Veritarach's live deployment (`https://157-230-241-156.sslip.io`), not fixtures. Generated by `scripts/generate_example_reports.py`.

- [veritarach_holdout.md](veritarach_holdout.md) — confusion matrix against Veracia's independently-built holdout set
- [veritarach_generalization.md](veritarach_generalization.md) — accuracy against 100 samples from a model outside Veritarach's training mix

There's no Hecate report here yet. Producing one needs a live Hecate deployment behind it (Postgres, a configured judge provider), and standing that up isn't something this repo does on its own -- the plugin and eval set are ready, but running it for real is a separate step against an actual Hecate instance.

## What these three turned up

The headline number doesn't hold up under independent testing. Veritarach's README reports 99.65% test F1; against Veracia's own 47-row holdout, the real figure is 0.62, with a false positive rate around 70% -- most of the human-written samples (19th-century prose from Austen, Doyle, Shelley, and Twain) got flagged as AI-generated.

That's not just "the number went down." Every single confidence score across all 47 holdout cases falls between roughly 0.50 and 0.69 -- including on inputs that aren't remotely ambiguous, like the opening line of *Pride and Prejudice* or a one-sentence definition of mitochondria written on the spot for a sanity check, both scored right at 0.54-0.56. A model that had actually learned to separate these classes should be confident and correct on cases that easy. Confidence never leaving a ~0.2-wide band around the decision boundary, on cases this unambiguous, points at weak separation between the classes in the live deployment, not just noise on hard examples.

The generalization run makes the pattern clearer rather than contradicting it. Accuracy on 100 samples from a locally-hosted Phi-4-mini (a model with no part in training) collapsed to 8%, but not because the model started calling things AI-generated less confidently -- it flipped almost entirely to predicting human_written instead (92 of 100 false negatives). Combined with the holdout skewing the other way (72% of predictions there came back ai_generated), the model isn't stuck on one answer; it's reacting to something in the text, just not to the thing that actually separates AI writing from human writing. The likeliest explanation is that it picked up surface stylistic cues specific to its three training providers and HC3's human half, and both 19th-century literary prose and a different model's writing style fall outside whatever it actually learned.

The adversarial check is the one place the deployed model comes closer to matching expectations: 15 of 18 already-correct predictions survived a paraphrase pass, and the average confidence drop was small (-0.009). But three samples flipped outright, and given how close to 0.5 confidence sits everywhere else, that's consistent with the same underlying issue -- decisions that are barely on one side of the line to begin with don't need much of a push to land on the other.

None of this says the training pipeline is broken -- 99.65% could be a real number on the exact distribution it was measured against. What it says is that number doesn't transfer to text outside that distribution, whether that's older or more formal human writing or an AI model the classifier never saw. Worth a closer look at whether the deployed checkpoint matches what training reported, and worth treating the 99.65% figure as a claim about in-distribution performance specifically, not general AI-text detection accuracy.
